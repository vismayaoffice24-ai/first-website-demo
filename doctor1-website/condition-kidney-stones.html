Kidney Stones (Nephrolithiasis)

Hard mineral deposits that crystallize within the renal collecting system, causing severe pain and potential obstruction to urinary flow.

What it is

Kidney stones (renal calculi) are solid mineral and acid salt deposits that crystallize and aggregate when urine becomes excessively concentrated. Stones can remain quiescent inside the renal calyces or travel downstream into the ureters—the narrow muscular tubes connecting the kidneys to the urinary bladder. While small stones (under 4 to 5 mm) frequently pass spontaneously with hydration and medical expulsive therapy, larger or obstructing stones can cause intense pain, urinary stagnation, infection, and potential back-pressure damage (hydronephrosis) to the kidney.

Common signs

The hallmark symptom of an obstructing kidney stone is renal colic: severe, sharp, cramping pain originating in the flank or lower back that radiates downward toward the lower abdomen, groin, or inner thigh. Other frequent signs include visible blood in the urine (hematuria, making urine look pink, red, or cola-colored), persistent nausea and vomiting, painful or burning urination (dysuria), a constant feeling of urinary urgency, passing cloudy or foul-smelling urine, and fever with chills if a secondary urinary infection has developed.

Common causes and risk factors

Inadequate daily fluid intake is the single most common trigger for stone formation, particularly in hot tropical climates where sweat losses concentrate urine. Additional contributing factors include high dietary intake of sodium, animal proteins, and oxalate-rich foods, metabolic abnormalities (such as hypercalciuria, hyperuricosuria, or hypocitraturia), recurrent urinary tract infections (leading to struvite stones), chronic gout (uric acid stones), hyperparathyroidism, certain medications, and a family history of kidney stone formation.

When to see a doctor

Seek medical care quickly if you experience severe, unbearable side or back pain, fever and chills accompanying flank pain (indicating a dangerous obstructed infection), repeated vomiting that prevents you from keeping liquids down, visible blood in the urine, or an inability to pass urine. Immediate attention prevents renal obstruction from causing lasting damage.

Frequently Asked Questions

How can I prevent kidney stones from forming again?

Drinking 2.5 to 3 liters of water evenly throughout the day is the single most effective preventive step. Depending on your stone composition (calcium oxalate, uric acid, or struvite), Dr. Neelima may also recommend reducing salt, moderating animal protein, and taking medications such as potassium citrate to normalize urinary pH.

Can kidney stones cause permanent kidney damage?

If a kidney stone lodges in the ureter and causes prolonged blockage (hydronephrosis), back-pressure can permanently damage delicate nephrons and lead to chronic kidney injury. Prompt diagnosis and relieving the obstruction protects long-term renal function.

Should I avoid calcium if I have calcium oxalate stones?

Contrary to common belief, reducing dietary calcium is usually not recommended. Eating a normal amount of dietary calcium actually binds oxalates in your digestive tract, preventing them from entering the bloodstream and reaching the kidneys.

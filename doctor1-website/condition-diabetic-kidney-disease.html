Diabetic Kidney Disease (Nephropathy)

Kidney damage linked to long-standing diabetes, characterized by progressive glomerular capillary thickening and albumin leakage into the urine.

What it is

Diabetic kidney disease—often referred to as diabetic nephropathy—develops when chronically elevated blood glucose levels cause microscopic structural and vascular injury to the kidney's filtering units (glomeruli). Over time, the glomeruli thicken and develop nodular scarring (Kimmelstiel-Wilson lesions). This impairs their ability to retain essential proteins in the blood, allowing albumin to escape into the urine (albuminuria), while gradually reducing the kidneys' capacity to clear metabolic toxins.

Common signs

During the earliest stages, diabetic kidney disease produces virtually no physical signs. As damage advances, patients may observe foamy or frothy urine caused by heavy protein leakage (macroalbuminuria), progressive swelling (edema) in the feet, ankles, lower legs, or hands, morning facial puffiness, worsening high blood pressure that becomes resistant to standard medications, fatigue and pale skin from renal anemia, loss of appetite, nausea, and frequent urination during the night (nocturia).

Why it matters

Diabetes is the leading cause of chronic kidney failure and end-stage renal disease worldwide. Because early diabetic nephropathy is silent, regular preventive laboratory screening offers the only dependable window of opportunity to detect microalbuminuria before permanent glomerulosclerosis sets in. When identified in its initial microalbuminuric phase, modern medical interventions can arrest or even reverse protein leakage and protect renal function for decades.

When to see a doctor

If you have Type 2 diabetes, you should have your urine albumin and blood creatinine checked at least once every year beginning from the time of diagnosis. If you have Type 1 diabetes, annual screening should begin within five years of diagnosis. See a nephrologist immediately if your lab reports show microalbuminuria (uACR above 30 mg/g), declining eGFR (below 60 mL/min), or if you develop unexplained swelling in your feet or face.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is microalbuminuria and why is it important?

Microalbuminuria means tiny traces of albumin (protein) are spilling into your urine (uACR between 30 and 300 mg/g). It is the earliest detectable warning flag of diabetic kidney involvement and the most critical stage where treatment can halt or reverse renal injury.

Can tight blood sugar control protect my kidneys?

Yes. Maintaining your HbA1c near your doctor's recommended target (typically around 7.0% for most adults) significantly reduces hyperfiltration injury and slows down the progression of diabetic nephropathy.

Do I need to stop certain diabetes medications if my kidneys decline?

Yes. As kidney filtering capacity (eGFR) declines, certain diabetes medications like metformin or sulfonylureas may need dosage adjustments or discontinuation to avoid accumulation. Dr. Neelima carefully reviews and adjusts your prescriptions.

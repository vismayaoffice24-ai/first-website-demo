Early Signs of Kidney Disease You Shouldn't Ignore

Published by Dr. Neelima · Nephrologist / Kidney Specialist, MIMS Hospital, Kannur

The kidneys are remarkably resilient organs, housing approximately two million microscopic filtering units called nephrons. Because remaining healthy nephrons can compensate for injured ones, kidney damage often advances quietly without noticeable discomfort. In fact, many individuals lose up to half of their renal function before severe warning symptoms emerge. Recognizing the initial, subtle signals of kidney distress is the most crucial step in preventing irreversible decline.

1. Persistent Swelling (Edema)

When the kidneys fail to remove excess sodium and water, fluid accumulates within tissues. This frequently manifests as tight shoes, swollen ankles and feet by the evening, or noticeable puffiness around the eyes upon waking in the morning. If pressure applied with your thumb leaves an indentation that persists for several seconds, medical evaluation is advised.

2. Changes in Urine Habits

Any alteration in your usual urinary pattern deserves clinical attention. Waking up multiple times throughout the night to pass urine (nocturia) can indicate that the renal tubules are losing their capacity to concentrate urine. Additionally, urine that appears frothy or bubbly—requiring repeated flushes—often points to excess protein (albumin) leaking past damaged glomerular filters. A reddish, tea-colored, or cloudy hue may indicate red blood cells or urinary tract irritation.

3. Unexplained Fatigue and Brain Fog

Kidneys produce a hormone called erythropoietin (EPO), which instructs the bone marrow to produce red blood cells. As renal function declines, EPO production drops, leading to anemia. Coupled with the buildup of metabolic waste in the bloodstream (uremia), patients often report persistent exhaustion, difficulty concentrating, or feeling chilly even in warm weather.

4. Dry, Itchy Skin and Metallic Taste

As mineral and nutrient balances shift, high levels of phosphorus and urea in the blood can trigger intense, deep-seated skin itching that lotions cannot relieve. Patients also frequently report that food tastes strange or metallic, accompanied by a loss of appetite for protein-rich meals.

When to Consult a Nephrologist

If you observe any of these symptoms—particularly if you have a history of diabetes or high blood pressure—a simple serum creatinine blood test and urine protein check can assess your kidney health. Early detection allows for medications, lifestyle adjustments, and protective strategies that slow or stop renal decline.

Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD)

A long-term, gradual loss of kidney function that can go unnoticed for years until detected on routine laboratory screenings.

What it is

Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) means the kidneys have sustained gradual, lasting damage, slowly losing their ability to filter metabolic waste products, regulate electrolytes, and excrete extra fluid from the bloodstream. CKD spans five progressive stages based on the estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate (eGFR), ranging from Stage 1 (mild kidney damage with normal eGFR above 90) to Stage 5 (kidney failure with eGFR below 15). Early diagnosis is crucial because appropriate clinical management can significantly retard progression and prevent complications.

Common signs

Early-stage CKD rarely produces noticeable symptoms because remaining healthy nephrons work harder to compensate. As filtering capacity declines further, patients may notice progressive fatigue and weakness (due to anemia and toxin buildup), persistent swelling of the feet, ankles, or under the eyes, foamy or frothy urine, increased urination at night (nocturia), muscle cramps, persistent metallic taste, loss of appetite, nausea, and dry, itchy skin.

Common causes and risk factors

The two leading causes of CKD in India are long-standing diabetes (diabetic nephropathy) and chronic high blood pressure (hypertensive nephrosclerosis). Other common causes include chronic glomerulonephritis (inflammation of the kidney's filtering units), polycystic kidney disease and other genetic disorders, recurrent kidney infections, long-standing urinary tract blockages (such as recurring kidney stones or enlarged prostate), regular unmonitored use of pain medications (NSAIDs), and a family history of kidney disease.

When to see a doctor

You should consult a nephrologist promptly if routine blood tests indicate elevated serum creatinine or an eGFR below 60 mL/min, if a urine test detects protein or albumin, or if you notice unexplained swelling in your lower limbs or face. Anyone with diabetes, chronic hypertension, or a family history of kidney disease should undergo comprehensive kidney screening at least once a year even if they feel completely healthy.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can chronic kidney disease be cured or reversed?

Chronic kidney disease typically involves permanent nephron scarring, which cannot be reversed. However, with timely diagnosis, lifestyle adjustments, and targeted medical therapy, progression can be substantially slowed or stabilized for many years, delaying or preventing the need for dialysis.

Does having CKD mean I will definitely need dialysis?

No. Many patients diagnosed with early or moderate CKD (Stages 1 through 3) who adhere to careful blood pressure, blood sugar, and dietary control live full, productive lives without ever progressing to Stage 5 or requiring dialysis.

What dietary changes are most important for CKD?

The primary dietary priorities include limiting daily sodium (salt) intake to under 2,000 mg, avoiding excess dietary protein, moderating potassium and phosphorus depending on blood lab levels, and maintaining appropriate hydration as guided by your nephrologist.

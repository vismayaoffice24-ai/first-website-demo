Acute Kidney Injury (AKI)

A sudden, rapid decline in renal filtering capacity occurring over a matter of hours or days, frequently treatable and reversible with timely medical intervention.

What it is

Acute Kidney Injury (AKI)—previously referred to as acute renal failure—is an abrupt, sharp decrease in the kidneys' ability to filter waste products, balance blood electrolytes, and maintain fluid equilibrium. Unlike chronic kidney disease, which develops slowly over years, AKI occurs rapidly over hours to a few days. It is often triggered during another severe illness, acute infection, or sudden fluid depletion, and in many instances, kidney function can significantly improve or fully recover if the underlying cause is promptly diagnosed and treated.

Common signs

Signs of AKI can vary depending on its severity. Common indicators include a marked reduction in urine volume (oliguria) or passing very little urine despite drinking liquids, sudden fluid retention causing rapid swelling in the legs, ankles, or feet, generalized weakness and exhaustion, nausea and vomiting, shortness of breath from fluid accumulating in the lungs, confusion or drowsiness, and in severe cases, chest pain or irregular heartbeats due to sudden potassium accumulation.

Common causes

Nephrologists classify AKI causes into three categories:
Pre-renal: Reduced blood flow reaching the kidneys, caused by severe dehydration (from prolonged vomiting, diarrhea, or heat stroke), severe blood loss, sepsis, or acute heart problems.
Intrinsic renal: Direct physical or toxic injury to renal tissue, resulting from nephrotoxic medications (such as NSAID painkillers, certain antibiotics, or IV radiocontrast dyes), acute glomerulonephritis, or severe systemic infections.
Post-renal: Sudden mechanical obstruction blocking urine outflow, such as large kidney stones lodged in the ureters, severe prostate enlargement, or pelvic tumors.

When to see a doctor

Seek medical attention immediately if you notice a sharp drop in urine output, sudden unexplained swelling, severe breathlessness, or persistent vomiting, particularly following an episode of food poisoning, high fever, surgery, or starting new medications. AKI is a medical emergency that requires immediate clinical assessment.

How a nephrologist can help

Dr. Neelima coordinates urgent diagnostic testing at MIMS Hospital, Kannur, including immediate blood electrolyte panels, serum creatinine and blood urea monitoring, urine analysis, and urgent renal ultrasound to rule out obstruction. Treatment focuses on stabilizing fluid balance, eliminating any offending medications, addressing underlying infections, and supporting renal perfusion. Frequently Asked Questions

Can kidneys fully recover after an episode of AKI?

Yes. Many patients experience complete or near-complete recovery of kidney function once the root cause (such as dehydration, an adverse medication reaction, or urinary obstruction) is resolved. However, some patients may be left with residual scarring, making long-term nephrology follow-up important.

Does AKI always require dialysis?

No. Most episodes of mild to moderate AKI are successfully managed with intravenous fluids, medication adjustments, electrolyte correction, and targeted medical therapy. Dialysis is reserved for severe instances where life-threatening fluid overload, severe acid-base imbalance, or dangerously high potassium occurs.

How can I prevent another episode of AKI?

Stay well hydrated—especially during hot weather or gastrointestinal illness—strictly avoid unprescribed painkiller (NSAID) overuse, inform healthcare providers of previous kidney injury before contrast imaging or surgery, and attend regular follow-ups.
